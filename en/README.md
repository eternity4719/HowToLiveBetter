# A High-Return Life Guide

On living longer and getting sick less, and on what to do when someone has an accident. On not wasting money, and on which things get people scammed or sued. On what you can apply for when you have no job and no money, and what registration a shop, a company or a website needs. Also on dating, marriage and children, on going abroad, and on learning a trade.<br>
635 pieces of advice. Each one states what it costs, what it buys back, how strong the evidence is, and cites nothing but journal papers and official documents.

You do not have to do all of it. This is a shortlist ordered by value for money, not a task list. Taking one or two entries with you counts. The author has not done most of them either.

## How to read it

- **You do not have to do all of it**: this is a shortlist ordered by value for money, not a task list. Taking one or two entries with you counts. Leave the rest and come back when you need them. The remark that this is easy to say and hard to do is fair. The author has not done most of it either. It is written down so that you can find it at the moment you need it. If you want the cheap ones, see "only the ones most worth doing" below.
- **If you want an AI to look things up**: the repository ships a Chinese skill ([skills/life-decision-guide](skills/life-decision-guide/)). Both Claude Code and Codex can install it. Once it is installed, ask it directly, for example "should I sign as a guarantor for a friend" or "is a two-hour daily commute worth it". It first pulls the relevant items out of the text, then answers, ranking them the way the book ranks things, and it says which section and which item the answer comes from. If it cannot find anything, it says so. It does not invent numbers. How to install it is in [the notes in that directory](skills/life-decision-guide/README.md).
- **If you want to filter by criteria**: open the [search page](https://streamentry.github.io/HowToLiveBetter/). You can filter by keyword, by section and by evidence grade. You can also filter on the three costs, money spent, time spent and willpower needed, and several filters can be combined. The text on the page is read straight from the files in `book/`. When the text changes, the page changes with it.
- **Items point at each other** (things like "see item 17 in section 8"). On the search page such a reference carries a dotted underline. Click it and the title and the "In plain language" line of the item it points to appear in place. To go there for real, press "Go there". If the filters happen to be hiding that item, the page clears the filters by itself. On GitHub, reading the text directly, these references are not clickable. But every one of them says what it points to, for example "see item 18 (write down the IOU when you lend money)". You know which item is meant without following it.
- **If you want to read in order**: within each section the items run from the best value for money to the worst. Start with the first few of each section.
- **If you want to read it offline or send it to someone**: download the [single-file offline HTML](https://github.com/streamentry/HowToLiveBetter/releases/download/epub-latest/HowToLiveBetter.html). The whole book, search and filters, is in that one file. Double-click it and it opens. No server, no network. You can send it straight through WeChat.
- **If you want to print it or read it on a phone**: download the [PDF](https://github.com/streamentry/HowToLiveBetter/releases/download/epub-latest/HowToLiveBetter.pdf). A4 layout, a bit over two hundred pages, with page numbers in the contents and with bookmarks. Each section starts on a new page.
- **If you want to read it on a Kindle or another reader**: download the [EPUB ebook](https://github.com/streamentry/HowToLiveBetter/releases/download/epub-latest/HowToLiveBetter.epub). Send it over with Send to Kindle and it is there.
- **All three are regenerated automatically each time the text changes**, and the download links never change. A copy you have forwarded will not update itself. The online version is the current one.
- **If the numbers do not make sense**: every item has an "In plain language" line. It turns the research wording in the "Benefit" field into everyday terms, such as "about a fifth lower chance of dying in the same period" or "a few days of detention and a fine of so much". It only uses what is already in the "Benefit" field and adds no new numbers. That one line is enough to decide on. The "Benefit" field keeps every number as it was, so read it if you want to check for yourself.
- **If you only want the firmest conclusions**: tick evidence grade A on the search page. That leaves the 422 items that carry a specific number and come from a meta-analysis or a large trial.
- **If you only want the ones most worth doing**: set value for money to "Exceptional". That gives the 108 items that cost no money, no time and no willpower, and whose benefit sits in the largest band. Add a "What it buys back" filter on top and you have a priority list for that measure.
- **Do not be thrown by section titles that begin with "Do not"**: a section title names the outcome that section is trying to prevent, such as not dying young or not wasting time. It does not mean every item under it is telling you not to do something. The action itself is in the item title. Every item title starts with a verb, so it already says whether the item is about doing something or about not doing something. Both appear in the same section. Section 4 has both "write 'I plan to' as 'at what time, where, and what I do when this comes up'" and "do not watch television or scrolling news". Read the item titles. Do not carry the tone of the section title over to them.

An item looks like this:

```markdown
### 5. Swap the salt in your home for low-sodium salt (potassium salt)
- Cost: A bag costs a few yuan more than ordinary salt. Swap it when you next buy, it takes no extra time. The taste barely changes.
- In plain language: In a randomised trial of 20,000 people, the people who swapped the salt in their home for low-sodium salt had about a 12% lower chance of dying within five years, and about 14% lower stroke risk. The two groups were assigned at random, so this is more trustworthy than a figure that comes only from following records.
- Benefit: A trial that assigned people at random to two groups, run in rural China, 20995 people, all of whom had had a stroke or were over 60 with high blood pressure, followed for 4.74 years. Result: the low-sodium salt group had about a 12% lower risk of death than the ordinary salt group (RR 0.88). Stroke about 14% lower (RR 0.86). Major cardiovascular events about 13% lower (RR 0.87).
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Neal B et al (2021). Effect of Salt Substitution on Cardiovascular Events and Death. NEJM. https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2105675
- Notes: Disputed. The people in the trial were high-risk elderly people, so a healthy young person gets much less out of swapping salt. If you have impaired kidney function, or you are taking a potassium-sparing drug, ask your doctor before you swap.
```

## The four resources

What this book wants to help you keep is not only lifespan. There are four things:

- **Lifespan**: live longer, and die less of things that could have been avoided
- **Time and energy**: do not spend the time you are alive on things that pay nothing back, and waste less of the day's attention and strength
- **Money**: spend less on things that were not worth the money, and put money where the return is certain
- **Personal freedom**: do not end up in a detention centre or a remand centre because you did not know where one line was

Every item answers two questions: what it costs (money / time / energy / willpower) and what it buys back (a change in all-cause mortality / a drop in a specific cause of death / time and energy saved / money saved / security and personal freedom). The items are ordered by value for money, not by category. Anything that costs almost nothing and buys back a lot goes at the top.

**Who receives the benefit of an item is graded.** The grades run from the highest, which is how likely the benefit is ever to come back to you personally, to the lowest: ① **you**; ② **your spouse and immediate family** (parents, children, grandparents, grandchildren); ③ **friends, colleagues and other relatives**, where the relationship goes both ways and a favour given now may come back later; ④ **strangers**, the lowest band, but not zero. The chance of a return is small, you do not know what the other person is like, and there is also a risk of being extorted, of being turned against and of revenge. The bands are not added together. When an item reaches band ④, the benefit and the risk are written out together.

Read the first aid section with this in mind. Of 38,227 out-of-hospital cardiac arrests in China, 79.2% happened at home. Learning to press is first of all so that you can press on your own family. Rules like "do not go into the water when you see someone drowning" and "do not step in to break up a fight" are self-protection rules in themselves. They keep you from turning from a bystander into the second person hurt. Whether to step in for a stranger is your own trade-off. The items set out the disclaimers, the self-protection steps and the risk, and they do not argue on your behalf that you have to do it.

The mortality numbers, the time and energy numbers, the money numbers and the legal consequences are counted separately and are not converted into each other. These four are the four "What it buys back" measures on the search page, and they are not compared against each other.

## Evidence grades

Every item carries an evidence grade:

| Grade | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| A | There is a specific number you can look up. The source is a meta-analysis pooling several studies, a large cohort followed for many years, or a randomised controlled trial (RCT). It can say how much lower the risk is (HR, RR, percentage drop) |
| B | There is research behind it, but no single definite number. Or it rests on a small sample, or on one study alone |
| C | The author's own experience, or a practice everyone accepts. No direct research literature |

Across the book's 635 items, 422 are grade A, 162 are grade B and 51 are grade C. A further 59 items are marked as disputed, and 2 carry a TODO for verification. A disputed item of grade A or B is marked "Disputed" and lists the evidence on the other side. Every source is an original document: a journal paper with a DOI or a PubMed link, or a report from an official body such as the WHO, the CDC or the National Bureau of Statistics. Secondhand retellings are not cited. A number that is not certain is marked "to be verified".

## Value for money

The evidence grade only answers whether a number can be trusted. It does not answer whether the thing is worth doing. So every item carries two more labels: the size of the benefit, which is how big the benefit is, and the measure, which is which kind of thing it buys back. The search page takes those two together with the three costs and works out a value-for-money band:

| Dimension | Values | How it is decided |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Measure | Lifespan / money / time and energy / personal freedom | Look at what the item mainly buys back. **Different measures are not compared with each other.** A 12% drop in all-cause mortality and 500 yuan saved a year are not on the same ruler, and there is no ranking between them |
| Size of the benefit | Large / medium / small | Follow the item's own "Benefit" field and apply thresholds settled in advance rather than going by feel. For lifespan, look at the percentage drop (≥20% is large, 10–20% is medium, under 10% is small, and so is something measured only on a surrogate marker and not on the final outcome). For money, look at the amount (tens of thousands of yuan is large, hundreds to thousands is medium, a few tens of yuan is small). For personal freedom, look at the consequence (avoiding criminal liability is large, avoiding detention or an administrative penalty is medium, avoiding a civil dispute is small). For time and energy, look at how much is saved (an hour a day is large, hours a week is medium, saving it once is small) |
| Value for money | Exceptional / high / moderate | A large benefit with all three costs at zero = exceptional. A large benefit with low costs, or a medium benefit with zero costs = high. Everything else = moderate |

Across the book's 635 items, 108 are exceptional (17%), 291 are high (46%) and 236 are moderate (37%). The middle band has the most items on purpose. The size of the benefit underneath it only has three levels, large, medium and small. Cutting it finer would be false precision.

**This band is the author's own judgement, not evidence.** By the standard of this book it counts as grade C in itself. It is a different thing from the evidence grade, and neither affects the other. An item can be grade A on evidence and only moderate on value (the shingles vaccine comes from a phase III RCT with 97.2% efficacy, but two doses cost three or four thousand yuan, and shingles rarely kills). An item can also be grade C on evidence and exceptional on value (send your itinerary to your family before you go abroad). Moderate does not mean do not do it. Every item in the book is recommended. This band only means that you have to weigh that expense yourself.

## Reading the numbers

The text sticks to everyday wording wherever it can, but a few statistical terms are unavoidable when a study is cited. Look them up in this table if you do not recognise one. On the search page, hovering over a dotted-underlined term (or tapping it on a phone) brings up the same explanation.

<details>
<summary>Show 41 terms (all-cause mortality, HR, RR, 95% CI, meta-analysis, BMI, LPR, earnest money and a deposit ...)</summary>

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| All-cause mortality | The share of a group of people who die over a period of time, whatever the cause. The book uses it to measure how long people live. Research papers call it all-cause mortality, abbreviated ACM |
| HR | Hazard ratio. Over the same period, how fast the group that did something had events (death, illness), divided by the group that did not. HR 0.87 means 13% lower than the group that did not do it, HR 1.21 means 21% higher |
| RR | Relative risk. The ratio of the chance of an event between two groups. The numbers read the same way as HR |
| OR | Odds ratio. Also a comparison between two groups, but the calculation differs a little from RR. When the event is rare, it is close to RR. When the event is common, it makes the gap look bigger than it is |
| IRR | Incidence rate ratio. The incidence rate is the share of people who newly get the disease over a period of time. Divide one group's rate by the other's. It reads the same way as RR |
| RaR | Rate ratio. It compares how many times something happened, for example how many falls, not how many people had an event. It reads the same way as RR |
| Standardised mortality ratio | Abbreviated SMR. How many people in this group actually died, divided by how many people of the same age would be expected to die. 5.86 means the group died 5.86 times as often as people the same age |
| Risk difference | Subtract the chance of an event in one group from the other, which gives the plain figure of how many extra cases there are per thousand. A ratio only says how many times higher it is. The risk difference says how many more people there actually are |
| 95% CI | 95% confidence interval. A figure from a study always carries some error, and this is the range the true value is likely to fall in. For a ratio, a range that crosses 1. For a figure with a plus or minus, a range that crosses 0. Either way the difference between the two groups may be chance, and the text then says "not statistically significant" |
| RCT | Randomised controlled trial. People are split into two groups by something like drawing lots, one group does the thing and one does not, and afterwards the results are compared. This is the most convincing way to show that the thing itself did the work |
| Meta-analysis | The results of many studies are pooled together and recalculated to give one overall figure |
| Cohort | A cohort study. A large group of people is picked out, followed for many years, and it is recorded who had an event. It can show that two things go together, but not that the first one caused the second |
| Observational | An observational study. The researcher only records from the side and does not assign who does what. Other factors can pull the result around, see confounding and reverse causation in this table, so read the numbers with some discount |
| Confounding | A third factor moves both the cause and the outcome at once, so that two things look causally related. People who eat a lot of vegetables often exercise more as well, and it is not clear which one is doing the work |
| Reverse causation | The direction of cause and effect is the wrong way round: it looks as if the first thing caused the second, when in fact the second caused the first. It is not that sleeping more makes you die early. It is that people who are seriously ill sleep more |
| d, g | Effect size, showing how big the gap between the two groups is. The gap is converted into how many times the usual amount of day-to-day variation it comes to: 0.2 counts as small, 0.5 as medium, 0.8 as large |
| r | Correlation coefficient. How closely two things move together, from -1 to 1. 0.1 counts as weak, 0.3 as medium, 0.5 as strong |
| MET | A unit of exercise intensity. Sitting quietly is 1 MET, brisk walking is about 3 to 4 MET. Written as MET·h it is intensity multiplied by the hours spent, which is the total amount of exercise |
| GRADE | A scoring system used around the world that rates how reliable a piece of evidence is, in four levels: high, moderate, low and very low |
| Intention to treat | When the effect is worked out, it is counted on the people who were told to go for screening, not on the people who actually went. Some of the people told never go, so this method makes the benefit of screening look smaller than it is for the people who do go |
| Pack-year | The unit for how much a person has smoked in total. Packs a day multiplied by the years smoked. 30 pack-years is a pack a day for 30 years |
| BMI | Body mass index, the usual number for weight against size: weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared |
| LDL | Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, usually called bad cholesterol |
| eGFR | Estimated glomerular filtration rate, a measure of how well the kidneys filter, in mL/min/1.73 m². The lower the number, the worse the kidney function, and the stages of chronic kidney disease are set by it |
| HBsAg | Hepatitis B surface antigen, one line on a lab report. A positive result means the hepatitis B virus has already been caught |
| HPV | Human papillomavirus. It has many types, and long-term infection with some of them leads to cervical cancer |
| LDCT | Low-dose chest CT. The radiation is about one fifth to one tenth of an ordinary CT |
| PM2.5 | Fine particles floating in the air, under 2.5 micrometres across |
| NOVA | A way of sorting food into four groups by how far it has been processed. "Ultra-processed food" is the fourth of them |
| LPR | The Loan Prime Rate, the benchmark for lending rates in China. It is published on the 20th of each month, and both the mortgage rate and the ceiling on interest in private lending are calculated from it |
| Final award | The result of labour arbitration takes effect at once, and the employer cannot take the same matter to court |
| Crude marriage rate, crude divorce rate | The number of marriages and divorces registered in the year, per thousand people. There is a third figure, the divorce-to-marriage ratio, which is the number of divorces divided by the number of marriages in the same year. It is not the same thing as the other two, so do not read them together |
| AED | Automated external defibrillator. The red or yellow emergency box common in public places. Switch it on and follow the voice prompts. Whether to deliver a shock is decided by the machine itself |
| CPR | Cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Press the chest hard when the heart has stopped, so that the blood keeps moving |
| 3C certification | China Compulsory Certification, China's mandatory product certification. A product on that list may not leave the factory and may not be sold unless it carries this mark |
| ICP filing | A registration formality that a website or app has to complete in the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology's system before it goes live |
| Classified protection | The classified protection system for network security. A system is sorted into one of several levels by how important it is, and the higher the level, the more security measures are required |
| GPL | An open-source licence. Something built with code of this kind usually has to release its own code as well when it is distributed |
| Non-compete clause | An agreement signed with the employer: for a period after you leave, you do not go to work for a competitor. The employer has to pay you monthly during that period, for at most 2 years |
| Subscribed capital | The money you promise to put in when you register a company. Once you have promised it, it has to be paid in within the period the law sets. It is not a number written down to look good |
| Earnest money and a deposit | Two words that differ by one character, and the difference in effect is large. 订金 is money paid in advance, with no penalty attached if someone changes their mind. 定金 carries a penalty: the party who took the money has to repay double if they change their mind, up to 20% of the contract value |

</details>
