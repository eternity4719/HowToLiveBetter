# 4. 不要浪费时间

本节只算时间账。看的是每天、每周、每年能省回多少小时，或者一件事按时做完的比例能提高多少。这里不把时间换算成寿命，也不换算成钱。涉及钱的地方只给算法，不给结论。本节多数条目的证据是单独一项研究，或者是时间利用调查的总量统计，所以等级以 B、C 为主。

### 1. Turn "I mean to do it" into "when, where, and if this comes up then that"
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: No money. It takes 1 to 2 minutes per thing to write one sentence of the form "if ... then ...". For example: "On Tuesday, when I sit down at my desk at 8 in the morning, I write the introduction first".
- In plain language: Rewrite "I am going to do this thing" as "on Tuesday, when I sit down at my desk at 8 in the morning, I write the introduction first". Pooling 94 tests, people who write it that way achieve their goals markedly more often, a medium to large effect. You only change how one sentence is worded. It costs no money and takes no effort.
- Benefit: Pooling 94 independent tests, people who first write the intention as "if ... then ..." reach their goals more often. This wording is called an implementation intention in psychology. Effect size d = 0.65, which counts as medium to large. Three things are doing the work: getting started, holding on when you are interrupted, and getting out of something that was never going to work.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Gollwitzer, P. M., & Sheeran, P. (2006). Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes. Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 38, 69–119. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1>
- Notes: This only works for things you genuinely want to do. If you do not want the goal in the first place, writing more sentences will not help. Also, most of these studies recruited students and mostly looked at health habits, so it may not work as well in a work setting.
### 2. Write down your exit condition before you start
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: No money. It takes 5 minutes when you start, to write clearly: "by this date, if I have not hit this mark, I stop". The hard part is honouring it on the day; stopping in front of other people loses face.
- In plain language: Before you start, write down one sentence: by this date, if I have not hit this mark, I stop. Pooling several studies, this wording really does help people withdraw from something that was never going to work. What you save is all the time you would otherwise have gone on putting in.
- Benefit: Pooling several implementation intention studies, the result is that a pre-set "if ... then ..." also helps people withdraw from something that was never going to work. The original wording is disengagement from failing courses of action. The time you save is the time you would otherwise have gone on investing.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Gollwitzer, P. M., & Sheeran, P. (2006). Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes. Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 38, 69–119. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1>
- Notes: Set the exit condition right at the start, while you are still calm. Set it later and the cost already sunk in will pull you. When you judge, look only at future input and future return, see item 3. Also, "being willing to withdraw" is only one sub-finding inside a pool of studies; nobody has worked out on its own how many hours it saves.
### 3. When deciding whether to continue, count only future input and future return, not what has already gone in
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: No money. The hard part is accepting one thing: the time you put in before is gone and cannot be recovered.
- In plain language: Money and time already spent make people keener to carry on. Pooling 98 sets of data, the tendency is reliably there. So when you judge whether to continue, count only how much more you have to put in and how much you can still get back. Leave out what you have already spent.
- Benefit: Money, energy and time already invested make people keener to continue. In psychology this is called the sunk cost effect. Pooling 98 sets of data, the analysis confirms that the tendency is reliably there. If you steer clear of it, you stop putting time in just because you "do not want to waste it".
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Arkes, H. R., & Blumer, C. (1985). The psychology of sunk cost. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 35, 124–140. <https://doi.org/10.1016/0749-5978(85)90049-4> ; Roth, S., Robbert, T., & Straus, L. (2015). On the sunk-cost effect in economic decision-making: A meta-analytic review. Business Research, 8(1), 99–138. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s40685-014-0014-8>
- Notes: The theatre experiment by Arkes and Blumer ran like this. The University of Ohio theatre sold season tickets and randomly assigned buyers to three prices: full price 15 dollars, 2 dollars cheaper, and 7 dollars cheaper. Over the first half of the season the full-price group attended an average of 4.11 shows, and the other two groups 3.32 and 3.29. Over the second half there was no clear difference between the three groups. Roth and colleagues found that the effect fades with time, and that older people are affected less.
### 4. Estimate the schedule from how long comparable tasks actually took, not from the plan
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: No money. Before estimating, spend a few minutes checking how long the last comparable job actually took.
- In plain language: Students estimated 33.9 days to finish their thesis and actually took 55.5 days, and only 29.7% finished within their own estimate. A second group was asked to recall how long the last comparable job had taken before estimating, and the share finishing on time rose from 29.3% to 60.0%.
- Benefit: Students expected 33.9 days to finish their thesis and actually took 55.5 days. Only 29.7% finished on time according to their own estimate. The researchers asked a second group to connect past experience to the task in front of them before estimating. In that group the share finishing on time rose from 29.3% in the control group to 60.0%. The optimism that shortens the estimate no longer showed up in that group.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Buehler, R., Griffin, D., & Ross, M. (1994). Exploring the "planning fallacy": Why people underestimate their task completion times. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 67(3), 366–381. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366> ; Flyvbjerg, B. (2006). From Nobel Prize to project management: Getting risks right. Project Management Journal, 37(3), 5–15. <https://doi.org/10.1177/875697280603700302> ; Halkjelsvik, T., & Jørgensen, M. (2012). From origami to software development: A review of studies on judgment-based predictions of performance time. Psychological Bulletin, 138(2), 238–271. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0025996>
- Notes: The Buehler study recruited only Canadian undergraduates. It is a single study with few participants, 37 people and about 40 per group. Estimating from the historical record of comparable projects is called reference class forecasting; Flyvbjerg explains how to do it in engineering. After pooling several studies, Halkjelsvik and Jørgensen found that underestimating is more common than overestimating, but by how much varies a lot with the task and the research method.
### 5. Send an agenda before the meeting and do not hold one without it; hold it standing if you can
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: No money. The organiser spends 5 to 10 minutes before the meeting writing an agenda. Holding a standing meeting needs the participants to go along with it.
- In plain language: In meetings where an agenda went out beforehand, participants were more likely to think the meeting was useful. In the experiment, meetings held sitting down ran 34% longer than those held standing, and the decisions the two formats produced were no different in quality.
- Benefit: Two surveys with 958 and 292 people respectively. The result is that in meetings where an agenda went out beforehand, participants were more likely to think the meeting had been effective, and the relationship holds statistically. In another experiment 55 groups held their meeting sitting down and 56 held it standing, and the sitting-down meetings ran 34% longer. The two formats produced decisions of no different quality.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Leach, D. J., Rogelberg, S. G., Warr, P. B., & Burnfield, J. L. (2009). Perceived meeting effectiveness: The role of design characteristics. Journal of Business and Psychology, 24(1), 65–76. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s10869-009-9092-6> ; Bluedorn, A. C., Turban, D. B., & Love, M. S. (1999). The effects of stand-up and sit-down meeting formats on meeting outcomes. Journal of Applied Psychology, 84(2). <https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.84.2.277>
- Notes: The standing-meeting experiment used tasks carried out by five-person groups in a laboratory. You cannot carry this conclusion over to long meetings and large conferences. Leach measured how effective participants felt the meeting was, not how much work actually got done.
### 6. Cut the number of meetings; do not meet when writing it down would do
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: No money. You need colleagues and your manager to go along with it. The hard part is that writing something clearly takes more effort than saying it out loud.
- In plain language: The more meetings you have in a day, the more tired you are and the more work you feel you have on your hands. Cut one meeting and the time you save is the full length of that meeting, with nothing deducted.
- Benefit: Two surveys of employees working more than 35 hours a week, with 676 and 304 people respectively. How much time meetings take does not have a fixed effect on work attitudes and well-being; it depends on two things: how well the meetings are run, and how much the job depends on other people. Another study had people keep a diary for a week, and the result was that the more meetings you had in a day, the higher the fatigue and the more work you felt you had done. The time you save equals the full length of the meetings you cut.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Rogelberg, S. G., Leach, D. J., Warr, P. B., & Burnfield, J. L. (2006). "Not another meeting!" Are meeting time demands related to employee well-being? Journal of Applied Psychology, 91(1). <https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.91.1.83> ; Luong, A., & Rogelberg, S. G. (2005). Meetings and more meetings: The relationship between meeting load and the daily well-being of employees. Group Dynamics: Theory, Research, and Practice, 9(1). <https://doi.org/10.1037/1089-2699.9.1.58>
- Notes:Disputed: Rogelberg 2006 found that the relationship between the number of meetings and well-being is not one-sided. In jobs that depend heavily on other people, many meetings are not a bad thing in themselves. Also, no study has compared writing things down asynchronously against holding meetings, so that part is the author's own inference.
### 7. Break a large task into sub-tasks, then estimate, then start
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: No money. The splitting itself takes 5 to 15 minutes.
- In plain language: When people estimate how long something will take, they do not automatically break the task up and count the pieces. Listing the sub-tasks first and estimating afterwards stops the estimate coming out too short. Once it is broken up, every small step can be started at once, and it is less easy to put off.
- Benefit: When people estimate how long something will take, they do not on their own split a task that has several parts into separate blocks. Writing a literature review, writing the discussion and sorting the references are already several jobs. Listing the sub-tasks first and estimating afterwards reduces the planning fallacy, the mistake of estimating a task too short. Once it is broken up, every small step becomes something you can begin at once, and the procrastination falls with it. The factors that predict procrastination best in the research include how much the task disgusts people and how far back the task has been pushed.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Kruger, J., & Evans, M. (2004). If you don't want to be late, enumerate: Unpacking reduces the planning fallacy. Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 40(5), 586–598. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2003.11.001> ; Steel, P. (2007). The nature of procrastination: A meta-analytic and theoretical review of quintessential self-regulatory failure. Psychological Bulletin, 133(1). <https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.1.65>
- Notes: How far to split: use "each sub-task can be finished in one sitting" as the test, because splitting too finely also costs time. Also, for Kruger and Evans, only the direction was verified from the abstract; the specific percentages were not checked against the full text.

### 8. Give things with no external deadline a date of your own
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: No money. The hard part is that once you set it you have to honour it; ignoring the day when it arrives means you never set it.
- In plain language: When someone else sets the deadline, 80.6% of students finish before it. When the deadline is their own estimate, only 38.7% finish on time. When something gets finished is decided almost entirely by the deadline rather than by your own estimate. So for anything with no deadline, set one yourself and honour it.
- Benefit: Among students with an external deadline, 80.6% finished before it. Among those working to their own expected time, only 38.7% finished on time. When something gets finished follows the deadline almost exactly, correlation coefficient r = 0.82, and the closer this number is to 1 the tighter the fit. The relationship with your own estimate is very weak.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Buehler, R., Griffin, D., & Ross, M. (1994). Exploring the "planning fallacy": Why people underestimate their task completion times. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 67(3), 366–381. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366>
- Notes: Tell other people the date you set and it behaves more like one they set. What the research actually verified is that deadlines set by others work. Self-set limits such as a time box or the Pomodoro technique have not been tested directly, so that part is an inference by analogy.
### 9. Read procrastination as avoiding something unpleasant, not as a lack of effort
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: No money. What changes is the direction of your judgement: first ask where in the task the unpleasant part is, then work out what to do.
- In plain language: Procrastination has little to do with laziness and little to do with personality. What tells you most about whether a person will put something off is how unpleasant the task itself is, how much they believe they can do it, and how easily they are pulled away by what is in front of them. So what helps is making the task less unpleasant and making the first step smaller, not telling yourself off and then forcing yourself through it.
- Benefit: A meta-analysis pooling 691 correlation coefficients found several things strongly and reliably related to procrastination: how much the task disgusts people, how far away the deadline is, self-efficacy (believing you can do it), impulsivity, and conscientiousness together with its components of self-control, distractibility, orderliness and achievement motivation. Neuroticism, rebelliousness and sensation seeking have only a very weak association with procrastination. The author explains this set of results with temporal motivation theory: how attractive something is right now falls off quickly as the reward moves further away.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Steel P (2007). The nature of procrastination: a meta-analytic and theoretical review of quintessential self-regulatory failure. Psychological Bulletin, 133(1), 65–94. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.1.65>
- Notes: The benefit is graded "medium" because these studies only give direction and do not measure how many hours are saved. The specific moves are in item 1 in this section (write "when, where, and if this comes up then that"), item 7 (break a large task into sub-tasks) and item 8 (give anything with no external deadline a date). These are pools of correlational studies, so they cannot show which causes which.
### 10. Put what you need within reach, move what you would rather not touch further away, and do not rely on resisting it there and then
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: No money, a few minutes each time. Put the phone in another room, and leave the book and the laptop open on the desk.
- In plain language: The further away something is, the less of it you take. Put the same snack further away and the amount eaten falls noticeably, a medium-sized effect. Offer fewer options and noticeably fewer people take them. These methods do not rely on you resisting in the moment; a few minutes of setting up is enough.
- Benefit: A Cochrane review including 24 randomised trials. Putting food further away lowers the amount eaten, standardised mean difference −0.60 (95% CI −0.84 to −0.36, the credible range; 12 studies, 1098 people, low-certainty evidence), which counts as a moderate effect. Reducing the options available shows a bigger drop in choosing that food, standardised mean difference −1.13 (95% CI −1.90 to −0.37, 3 studies, 154 people, low-certainty evidence). The regression analysis shows: the further away it is and the fewer the options, the stronger the effect.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Hollands GJ, Carter P, Anwer S, et al. (2019). Altering the availability or proximity of food, alcohol, and tobacco products to change their selection and consumption. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 9, CD012573. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD012573.pub3>
- Notes: The reason for grading B: all 24 studies in this review are about food, with not one on alcohol or tobacco; 14 were done in laboratories, and all come from high-income countries. So applying "move it further away and you touch it less" to phones and games is an extension in the same direction, with no direct trial. Putting the phone out of sight has been tested directly, see item 1 in section 3 (turn off notifications you do not need, and keep the phone out of sight while you work).
### 11. To make yourself follow through, put money on it, but first ask whether you are willing to stake any
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: A deposit scheme requires you to put up your own money first, and it is forfeited if you do not deliver. The trial staked 150 dollars.
- In plain language: If you want to force yourself to deliver, you can take a bonus for delivering, or you can put up a deposit that is refunded only if you deliver, plus a bonus. Among people willing to take part in either, those who put up a deposit delivered 13 percentage points more often. But only 13.7% were willing to put up a deposit, against 90% willing to take the bonus, so a deposit only works for people willing to stake money.
- Benefit: A US randomised trial of 2538 people randomly assigned employees and their family and friends to four incentive programmes or to usual care. The reward scheme (about 800 dollars on delivery) had an acceptance rate of 90.0%, and the deposit scheme (150 dollars up front, plus a 650 dollar reward) 13.7%. Six-month continuous smoking abstinence: the four incentive programmes ran between 9.4% and 16.0%, against 6.0% for usual care. The reward scheme 15.7%, the deposit scheme 10.2%. But among just the 13.7% willing to take part in either, the deposit scheme gave a six-month abstinence rate 13.2 percentage points higher than the reward scheme (95% CI 3.1 to 22.8, the credible range).
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Halpern SD, French B, Small DS, et al. (2015). Randomized trial of four financial-incentive programs for smoking cessation. New England Journal of Medicine, 372(22), 2108–2117. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1414293>
- Notes: This trial measured smoking cessation; applying it to writing a thesis or going to the gym is an extension in the same direction, so the benefit is graded "medium". When you pick a scheme, first check whether you will really use it. A method most people refuse has no use for them, however strong the effect. So pick the one you are willing to accept; staking a smaller amount still beats not staking. For how to give up smoking, see item 3 in section 2 (cessation drugs).
### 12. Count a new habit in months, not in "21 days"
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: No money. What it takes is repeating the same action every day in the same setting, for several months.
- In plain language: "You can build a habit in 21 days" has no basis behind it. A study following people for 12 weeks worked out that an action becomes something you do without thinking in as little as 18 days, and in 254 days in the slow cases. Two or three weeks without it becoming easier is normal. Missing a day in between does not reset your progress.
- Benefit: 96 volunteers each picked one eating, drinking or activity action, did it every day in a fixed setting (such as "after breakfast") for 12 weeks, and rated how automatic it felt each day. 82 people's data were good enough to analyse, and the model fitted successfully for 62 of them. The time taken to reach 95% of each person's own automaticity ceiling ranged from 18 days to 254 days. The study also found that missing one opportunity to do the action had no substantial effect on how the habit formed.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Lally P, van Jaarsveld CHM, Potts HWW, Wardle J (2010). How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world. European Journal of Social Psychology, 40(6), 998–1009. <https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674>
- Notes: There is only this one study, 96 people, and the actions were all simple ones (such as eating a piece of fruit after a meal). How long a complicated thing takes, such as running three times a week or writing every day, has not been tested. So still finding it hard after two weeks is not evidence of failure. To make the repetition actually happen, tie the action to a fixed setting, see item 1 in this section (write "when, where, and if this comes up then that").
### 13. When procrastination already affects your life, use self-help materials backed by randomised trials rather than insisting on finding a therapist
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=中 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: A self-help book costs a few tens of yuan, and some are free. It takes about ten weeks of work: one section to read and one exercise a week.
- In plain language: Procrastination severe enough to cause suffering can be treated. Ten weeks of working through cognitive behavioural self-help material works clearly better than doing nothing, whether someone guides you or you do it entirely on your own. Guided and unguided make no visible difference, so working from the material yourself is effective too.
- Benefit: 150 people were randomly split into three groups: guided self-help with a therapist, unguided self-help, and a waiting-list control. After ten weeks, the between-group effect size on the pure procrastination scale was d = 0.70 for the guided group (95% CI 0.29–1.10, the credible range) and d = 0.50 for the unguided group (0.10–0.90); on the irrational procrastination scale they were 0.81 (0.40–1.22) and 0.69 (0.29–1.09). The share reaching a clinically meaningful improvement: 31.3% to 40.0% in the guided group and 24.0% to 36.0% in the unguided group. The two groups did not differ significantly on any outcome.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Rozental A, Forsell E, Svensson A, Andersson G, Carlbring P (2015). Internet-based cognitive-behavior therapy for procrastination: A randomized controlled trial. Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 83(4), 808–824. <https://doi.org/10.1037/ccp0000023>
- Notes: The participants were Swedish adults using Swedish-language material, and there is no ready-made equivalent in Chinese, so the benefit is graded "medium". The components are the usual ones: behavioural activation, behavioural experiments, stimulus control, and material on motivation and working methods. Stimulus control is item 10 in this section (move what you would rather not touch further away). When procrastination comes with clear low mood or anxiety, start with item 19 in section 3 (move, get daylight, sleep on time, talk to someone, call 12356).
### 14. Use your own hourly rate to decide which housework to outsource
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=多 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: You have to pay for the outsourcing, and how much depends on the service you buy. You also have to spend time once working out your own hourly rate.
- In plain language: People in China spend 1 hour 17 minutes a day on housework on average. Among the people who actually did some that day, it was 1 hour 59 minutes. People who spend money to buy time report higher life satisfaction. The same amount of money spent on buying time makes people happier in the moment than the same amount spent on buying things.
- Benefit: Residents of China spend 1 hour 17 minutes a day on housework on average. Counting only the people who did housework, the average is 1 hour 59 minutes. These are 2024 survey figures, covering 38,500 households and 107,000 people. A separate study took a sample of 6271 people in four countries and found higher life satisfaction among people who spend money to buy time. The researchers also ran an experiment in real life: the same amount of money spent on buying time brought more happiness in the moment than the same amount spent on physical things.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Whillans, A. V., Dunn, E. W., Smeets, P., Bekkers, R., & Norton, M. I. (2017). Buying time promotes happiness. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1706541114> ; National Bureau of Statistics (国家统计局) (2024). Bulletin of the Third National Time Use Survey（第三次全国时间利用调查公报（第二号））. <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202410/t20241031_1957216.html>
- Notes: Work out your hourly rate first. The formula is one month's take-home income ÷ the hours you actually work in a month. Then see what it would cost to outsource this job. Outsource it when the price is below your hourly rate and you will really spend the saved time on earning money or resting. Everyone's hourly rate and every service price differs, so work out the conclusion yourself. Also, Whillans measured whether buying time made people happy, not how many hours were saved.

### 15. Set a hard limit on short videos and mindless scrolling
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: No money. The actions take only a few minutes: turn off notifications, delete the app, or set a daily time limit on it. The hard part is keeping your hand off it and resisting the tap.
- In plain language: People in China spend 5 hours 37 minutes a day online on average. Among those who actually went online that day, it was 6 hours 3 minutes. In 2018, on the same method, it was only 2 hours 42 minutes. This is the largest figure in this section. Cutting one hour a day gives you 365 hours a year.
- Benefit: Residents of China spend 5 hours 37 minutes a day online on average. Counting only those who actually went online that day, the average is 6 hours 3 minutes. 92.9% of people used the internet on the day. These are 2024 figures. In 2018, on the same method, it was only 2 hours 42 minutes. This is the largest figure in this section. Cutting one hour a day out of it gives you 365 hours a year. Separately, according to the China Internet Network Information Center (中国互联网络信息中心), by June 2025 internet users spent an average of 30.6 hours a week online, 1.9 hours more than half a year earlier. There are 1.068 billion short video users, 95.1% of internet users.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: National Bureau of Statistics (国家统计局) (2024). Bulletin of the Third National Time Use Survey（第三次全国时间利用调查公报（第二号））. <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202410/t20241031_1957216.html> ; National Bureau of Statistics (国家统计局) (2019). Bulletin on the 2018 National Time Use Survey（2018年全国时间利用调查公报）. <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202302/t20230203_1900224.html> ; China Internet Network Information Center (中国互联网络信息中心) (2025). The 56th 《中国互联网络发展状况统计报告》 (Statistical Report on the Development of China's Internet). <https://www.cnnic.net.cn/NMediaFile/2025/0730/MAIN1753846666507QEK67ZS9DH.pdf>
- Notes: Time online includes work, study and socialising, and it is not all waste. It is graded C because no study has worked out how much is saved after a limit, or where that time then goes; all that is available is the aggregate total. For the "seeing how other people are doing" part of this, randomised trials have measured how much is saved and how much mood changes: see item 21 in section 3.
### 16. No television and no doomscrolling; take in the news you need at fixed times
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: No money. The hard part is breaking the habit of opening it on impulse, and you may miss some breaking news.
- In plain language: People over 15 in the United States watch 2.6 hours of television a day, which takes half of all their leisure time. ### 17. Spend time once learning the keyboard shortcuts and automation of the software you use every day
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=中 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: No money. You have to put in a few hours to several dozen hours of learning in one go. The hard part is that at first you are slower than with the mouse.
- In plain language: Most people who have used Word for years still click the toolbar icons. In an experiment comparing groups, shortcuts were faster than both menus and icons. Learning this once is a one-off cost, and after that every single operation each day pays it back. The longer you do the same kind of work, the more it pays back.
- Benefit: The study looked at 251 experienced Word users, most of whom rarely used the faster shortcuts and still worked mainly by clicking the icon toolbar. The experiment comparing groups confirms that of the three methods, menus, icons and shortcuts, shortcuts are the fastest. The time saved accumulates a little with every operation each day. People who do the same kind of work for a long time gain the most.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Lane, D. M., Napier, H. A., Peres, S. C., & Sándor, A. (2005). Hidden costs of graphical user interfaces: Failure to make the transition from menus and icon toolbars to keyboard shortcuts. International Journal of Human-Computer Interaction. <https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327590ijhc1802_1>
- Notes: Only learn the operations you will use more than ten times a day; learning everything is a different kind of waste. To be clear, that efficiency experiment had only 6 participants, and the abstract does not give the seconds saved per operation.
### 18. Put commute time first when you choose where to live, and shorten the one-way journey
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=多 时间=中 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: The cost is high. You may have to pay more rent, or move to a smaller place. Moving itself takes time.
- In plain language: The longer your commute, the worse you feel your life is going. That loss is not made up in salary or housing. Cutting 30 minutes off each one-way journey gives you 5 hours a week.
- Benefit: Residents of China spend 50 minutes a day on transport on average. Counting only those who travelled on the day, it is 1 hour 2 minutes. These are 2024 figures. German data following the same group of people over the long term shows that the longer your commute, the stably lower your subjective well-being (how well you feel life is going). And that loss is not made up in income or housing. A review bringing several studies together also confirms that the longer the commute, the less satisfied people are with it, and this holds whatever mode of transport they use. Cutting 30 minutes off each one-way journey gives about 5 hours a week.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Stutzer, A., & Frey, B. S. (2008). Stress that doesn't pay: The commuting paradox. Scandinavian Journal of Economics, 110(2), 339–366. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9442.2008.00542.x> ; Chatterjee, K., et al. (2020). Commuting and wellbeing: A critical overview of the literature with implications for policy and future research. Transport Reviews, 40(1), 5–34. <https://doi.org/10.1080/01441647.2019.1649317> ; National Bureau of Statistics (国家统计局) (2024). Bulletin of the Third National Time Use Survey（第三次全国时间利用调查公报（第二号））. <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202410/t20241031_1957216.html>
- Notes:Disputed: after pooling several studies, Chatterjee and colleagues found no stable relationship between commute length and overall life satisfaction. They argue that people can broadly trade a long commute back for better work or housing. Also note that the transport activity figures count all travel, not just commuting. And if you can travel outside the peak hours, the same journey takes less time.
