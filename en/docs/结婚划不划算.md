# Is marriage worth it: splitting one muddled bill into five clear ones

Many people ask one broad question: is marriage a loss or a gain. That question packs several different things together. Can the other person give you emotional support. Will they share the housework. Can they earn money. And do you have to get married to keep elderly relatives off your back. You cannot convert these into a common unit, so they cannot be answered together. This essay takes them apart. Each of the five bills uses only figures from official statistics or from a meta-analysis, which pools several studies together. Where a figure cannot be worked out, this essay says so. The last section is a list you can fill in yourself. Nothing here says whether you should get married.

## 1. Take the question apart

"Is marriage worth it" holds at least five separate bills. They use different units, they are worked out in different ways, and they cannot be added together.

1. Money: first the betrothal gifts, the wedding and the marital home, which you pay for out of pocket. Second, what the law says about who owns the property. There are no official statistics on the first. The second is set out in plain provisions.
2. Time: housework, caring for family members, making allowances for the other person's work. These are hours of work that nobody pays for, and the unit is minutes per day.
3. Health: how much the death rate differs between married people and unmarried people. This bill can only show that two things occur together. It cannot show that marriage causes the gap.
4. Legal risk: how property is divided, how long it takes to get out, and which contributions can be clawed back as a one-off payment. This bill turns on what the provisions say.
5. Quality of the relationship: whether the other person gives you emotional support, and how badly the two of you fight. This bill can only be measured by the strength of the associations found in research. It does not convert into money, and it does not convert into time.

The fifth bill is the hardest to turn into numbers, and it is the closest to what most people want to know.

## 2. The data and sources behind each bill

### Registration data: the marriage rate and the divorce rate are two crude rates

The figures come from the Ministry of Civil Affairs, 2024 Statistical Bulletin on the Development of Civil Affairs. In 2024, 6,106,000 couples registered marriages in line with the law nationwide, 20.5% fewer than the year before, and the marriage rate was 4.3 per 1,000. 3,513,000 couples completed divorce formalities in line with the law, and the divorce rate was 2.5 per 1,000. Of those 3,513,000 couples, 2,622,000 registered the divorce with a civil affairs department and 891,000 had it ordered or mediated by a court. The note in the bulletin sets out how the two rates are worked out. Take the number of marriages (divorces) that year, divide it by the average total population for that year, then multiply by 1,000, which gives a per-mille rate.

Both rates have the whole population as their denominator, which is why they are called crude rates. "Crude" means no breakdown of any kind. They answer the question of how many couples per 1,000 people registered a marriage or a divorce this year. They do not answer whether a marriage will end in divorce. Answering that needs data that follows the same group of people for a long time. This essay did not find such data in the official text.

### The health bill: married people have a lower death rate, but that is association, not causation

Three meta-analyses each pooled many studies together, and they give the same order of magnitude. "All-cause mortality" below means every death put together, whatever the cause.

- Manzoli et al (2007) pooled 53 comparisons and more than 250,000 elderly people. "Not in a marriage" here covers three kinds of people: the widowed, the divorced or separated, and those who have never married. Compared with these three groups, married people had an all-cause mortality RR of 0.88, about 12% lower. RR is the ratio of the death risk in the two groups, and a value below 1 means the married side is lower. 95% CI 0.85–0.91 is the credible range for this figure. Looked at separately, the three groups come out at 1.11 for the widowed, 1.16 for the divorced or separated and 1.11 for the never married, which are about 10%, 16% and 10% higher. The result does not change when men and women are separated, when better and weaker studies are separated, or when Europe and the Americas are separated. Recalculated with the most conservative method, the RR is 0.94, only about 6% lower.
- Roelfs et al (2011) pooled 95 papers and more than 500 million people. People who had never married had an all-cause mortality HR of 1.24 compared with married people, about 24% higher. HR is a multiple in the same way as RR, and a value above 1 means higher risk. 1.19–1.30 is the credible range for this figure. The older a person is, the smaller the gap. The more rigorous the study, the smaller the gap it calculates.
- Wang et al (2020) pooled 21 studies and more than 7,890,000 people. Being outside a marriage was associated with all-cause mortality, cancer mortality and cardiovascular mortality, in men as well as women. The association was stronger in men for cardiovascular mortality and all-cause mortality. Never-married men had a 31% higher risk of dying from a stroke and a 9% higher all-cause mortality than never-married women.

All three studies only followed records of groups of people, with no control group. People who are healthier, better paid and better socially connected are both more likely to get married and more likely to live longer. That is called selection effect. The Manzoli paper also found signs of publication bias. Publication bias means studies with attractive results are more likely to get published, so a pooled figure leans optimistic. These numbers can only be read as married groups having a somewhat lower death rate. They cannot be read as "get married and you will die 12% less". The samples in these studies are mostly European and American, so in China they serve as an order of magnitude and nothing more.

### The time bill: the gender gap in unpaid labour is an upper-bound reference

The figures come from the National Bureau of Statistics, Third National Time Use Survey, fielded in May 2024. Three terms first. Unpaid labour is the part of the work that nobody pays for. "Participants" means the people who really did that thing on the day. "Participation rate" means the share of people who did that thing on the day. The results are like this. Participants in unpaid labour spent an average of 2 hours 45 minutes a day. Men spent 1 hour 52 minutes and women 3 hours 29 minutes. The participation rate was 75.6%, with 67.5% for men and 83.9% for women. Housework taken on its own works out at 1 hour 17 minutes a day averaged over all residents. Counting only the people who really did it that day, it is 1 hour 59 minutes, 28 minutes less than in 2018, and the participation rate is 64.9%. Caring for family members taken on its own works out at 30 minutes a day averaged over all residents. Counting only the people who really did it that day, it is 1 hour 46 minutes, and the participation rate is 28.4%.

The second survey, in 2018, came out like this. Housework averaged over all residents was 1 hour 26 minutes, with 45 minutes for men and 2 hours 6 minutes for women. The participation rate was 40.4% for men and 75.6% for women. Caring for the daily lives of children averaged over all residents was 36 minutes, with 17 minutes for men and 53 minutes for women. The participation rate was 12.3% for men and 25.1% for women.

Both surveys break the figures down only by sex, by urban or rural area, and by age. Neither breaks them down by whether someone is married. So there is no official figure for "how much more housework do you do a day after getting married". What you can use is the gap between men and women. In 2024 the gender gap in unpaid labour was 1 hour 37 minutes. If the division of housework was never settled, that is about as large a gap as the two of you could end up with.

### The money bill: default rules, written agreements and payment for housework

There are no official statistics on betrothal gifts, weddings or the marital home, so this essay gives no figures for them. What can be set out clearly are the rules under the marriage and family part of the Civil Code. The Civil Code took effect on 1 January 2021.

- Article 1062: four kinds of things you obtain between the wedding day and the divorce day count as joint property of the two of you. First, wages, bonuses and remuneration for work. Second, the returns from business and investment. Third, the returns from intellectual property. Fourth, property inherited or given to you by someone else. The fourth kind has one exception. If a will or a gift contract names only one of you, it is not joint property. Both people have equal power to dispose of joint property.
- Article 1063: four kinds of things count as personal property and do not go into joint property on divorce. First, property you already had before the marriage. Second, compensation or payments for injury to the body. Third, property that a will or a gift contract names as belonging to one side only. Fourth, daily necessities for the exclusive use of one person.
- Article 1065: the two of you can agree for yourselves who owns the property before and after the marriage. You can agree that each keeps their own. You can agree that everything is held in common. You can agree that part is kept separately and part is held in common. The agreement has to be in writing, and once written it binds both of you in law. If there is no agreement, or the agreement is unclear, the two rules above apply.
- Article 1088: if one of you has put in more than the other in looking after the children, caring for the elderly or helping the other with their work, then on divorce you can ask the other for a payment in compensation. How much goes to the two of you to agree first, and a court decides if you cannot agree.

This set of rules is useful to both sides. If you are the one who earns more, read Article 1062 and Article 1065 closely. If you are the one who does more at home, read Article 1088 closely. A property agreement only has to be in writing. The law does not ask you to have it notarised at a notary office.

### Exit cost: the cooling-off period and the grounds for going to court

- Article 1076: if both of you want a divorce, first sign a written divorce agreement, then apply in person at the marriage registration office. The agreement has to say who looks after the children and how property and debts are handled. All of this has to be what the two of you have settled on.
- Article 1077: within the thirty days counted from the day the registration office receives the application, either party who changes their mind can withdraw the application. These thirty days are what people call the cooling-off period. In the thirty days after the cooling-off period ends, the two of you have to apply in person for the divorce certificate. If you do not go, the application is treated as withdrawn.
- Article 1079: if one of you wants a divorce and the other does not agree, you can go to mediation first, or you can sue in court directly. Once the case is in court, the court also has to try mediation first. If the court is satisfied that the marriage has genuinely broken down and mediation does not work, it should grant the divorce. In the situations below, where mediation has failed, the divorce should be granted: bigamy or living with someone else, domestic violence or abuse and abandonment, bad habits such as gambling and drug use that repeat after repeated admonition, and two years of separation because the two of you no longer get along. There is one further situation. If a first decision refuses the divorce, and the two of you then live apart for a full year, a second case should end in a divorce.

Of the 3,513,000 couples who divorced in 2024, 891,000 went through a court judgment or court mediation. The cooling-off period only covers a divorce registered with the civil affairs department. It does not apply to a divorce fought in court.

### Quality of the relationship: a bill worth more than whether you get married

Robles et al (2014) pooled 126 studies and more than 72,000 people. The conclusion is that the higher the quality of a marriage, the better the health. How closely two things travel together is measured with a number called the effect size r. The closer r is to 0, the more nearly the two things are unrelated. The larger the absolute value of r, the more closely they travel together. A minus sign in front of r means that when one is high, the other is low. The r values for these associations run from 0.07 to 0.21. With risk of death, r = 0.11. With the cardiovascular response when the two of you are in conflict, r = −0.13. None of these values is large. But the association between health behaviours such as eating well and health is of the same order. Part of the results show publication bias, because attractive results are easier to get published. Some studies tested for a difference between men and women and found almost none. Most of these studies only followed records, with no control group, so no cause can be drawn from them. The cardiovascular review by Dhindsa et al (2020) also points out that how satisfied you are with a marriage and how good the marriage is have a clear effect on cardiovascular risk.

This essay did not find a meta-analysis that directly compares mortality in "a marriage that goes badly" with mortality in "not being married". So "a bad marriage is worse than not marrying" is at present a conclusion drawn by inference, with no figures behind it. What holds up is this. The difference between "married or not" is mixed up with selection effect, and "how good the marriage is" is a separate matter. If you only look at whether you are married and never at how you are getting on, you lose the whole quality-of-relationship bill.

## 3. How to work it out for yourself: a list to fill in

Fill in one bill at a time. Do not add the numbers from different bills together. Write "do not know" in any box you cannot fill in. "Do not know" is useful information in its own right.

What the other person brings (fill in one line at a time)

- Money: how much the other person earns, how much they owe, and what property they had before the marriage. For property after the marriage, do you follow the default rules of the law, or do you write an agreement? If you write an agreement, what goes in it.
- Time: how much unpaid housework and care does the other person do a day at the moment. The national average is there for comparison. Among people who really did it on the day, men spent 1 hour 52 minutes and women 3 hours 29 minutes. How these jobs get split after you marry. How they get split when there are children and elderly relatives who need looking after. Whose job in the two of you can be put off.
- Quality of the relationship: how many times the two of you have clashed in the past half year, and how each one ended. What the other person did when you were ill, out of work or low in spirits. And the other way round, what you did when those things happened to them.
- Emotional value: when you are with the other person, do you come away with more energy or less. You can only tell this after a few months together. A list of conditions will not show it.

What you put in (fill in one line at a time)

- Money: how much you pay out for the marriage itself, including betrothal gifts, the wedding and the marital home. If you did not marry, where would that money go instead.
- Time: estimate how much more housework and care you do a day. Then estimate how much more time you spend a week on social obligations.
- Health: whether your sleep, your eating and your exercise get better or worse because of the marriage. That is closer to you than any figure a meta-analysis produces.
- Exit cost: for a registered divorce you wait out the 30-day cooling-off period and then go and collect the certificate within the following 30 days. For a court case you have to meet one of the situations in Article 1079. Can you carry that period.

How the law protects you (applies to both people)

- Whether there is evidence for the property there was before the marriage, such as a purchase contract, bank statements, a gift contract or a will. Can that evidence show that it was "there before the marriage", or that it "names one side only".
- Whether to sign a written property agreement. Whether it covers only property from before the marriage, only what is earned after it, or both ends.
- Who does more of the housework and care after the marriage. Does the one who does more know that Article 1088 allows a payment in compensation. Are the two of you willing to agree the method in advance.
- Debt: money borrowed after the marriage in one person's own name, and which of it counts as a joint debt of the two of you. Does the other person know about that money.

Fill in the column on marrying to please elderly relatives on its own

- What the elders get: peace of mind, a face that saves out, no more being pushed to marry, and someone to rely on for support in old age.
- Which of these are real benefits to the elders and which are really benefits to you.
- You carry the cost in all three rows above. The benefits the elders get do not turn into your health on their own, and they do not turn into the quality of your relationship on their own.
- Once you have written it out this way, whether this bill is heavy or light is for the person filling it in to judge.

## 4. Common mistakes

Mixing up the two divorce rates. One is the crude divorce rate, which counts how many couples divorce per 1,000 people, and in 2024 it was 2.5 per 1,000. The other is the "divorce-to-marriage ratio", which divides the number of divorces that year by the number of marriages that year, 3,513,000 / 6,106,000 ≈ 57.5%. The top and the bottom of that ratio are not the same group of people. Most of the people divorcing this year married many years ago. When there are fewer marriages, the ratio climbs on its own, so it cannot show that "half of marriages end in divorce". The bulletin from the Ministry of Civil Affairs has no such indicator either.

Taking "occur together" for "so therefore". Married people have a death rate about 12% lower, and that is a result of pooling several studies together. But being healthy, well paid and well socially connected already affects both things at once: whether you can get married, and whether you can live long. This bill can be worked out, but read it at a discount, and it cannot take the place of a judgement about the relationship in front of you.

Treating marriage as a task to be completed. As a task, you watch only the state of "married or not" and never "how you are getting on". But the effect of relationship quality on health is independent of whether you are married, and a decision made as a task usually loses that whole bill.

Taking the national average for your own figures. The gender gap in the time use survey is a national average. The property rules are the default settings the law provides, and a written agreement can change them. National figures give you an order of magnitude and a starting point for negotiating. They do not give you a conclusion.

Where there is no official figure, do not fill the gap with a figure from somewhere else. Betrothal gifts, weddings and the cost of raising children have no official statistics, so this essay leaves them blank. Filling in your own real numbers beats quoting any organisation's estimate.
