# How the body tells the time, and why night shifts hurt

This is the long-form version of item 39 in section 2 (the longer you work night shifts, the higher your cardiovascular risk). That item only works out the arithmetic. This essay sets out the reasoning behind it.

The question to answer is whether the body really does know what time it is. If it does, why is working night shifts not a matter of getting used to it after a few goes?

## 1. There really is a timekeeping organ in the body

In the hypothalamus of the human brain there is a small cluster of neurons called the suprachiasmatic nucleus, about twenty thousand cells. It is the master clock of the whole body.

It keeps time through a set of "clock genes" that inhibit one another. Two proteins called CLOCK and BMAL1 switch on the transcription of two other genes, PER and CRY. Once the proteins made from PER and CRY have built up, they switch off the pair of proteins that switched them on. When these proteins have broken down, the cycle begins again. One turn of it takes about 24 hours.

So this clock does not work out the time by watching the outside world. Put a person in a room with no light at all, no clock and no social cues, and the rhythm carries on regardless. It is only that the cycle is not exactly 24 hours and drifts a little each day. The 2017 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine went to the three researchers who found this molecular mechanism.

## 2. This clock sets itself by light, and the route it uses is not the one that does the seeing

In the retina, apart from the cells that form the image, there is a separate class of cells that sense light. They contain a pigment called melanopsin, which takes no part in seeing anything clearly and only passes the strength of the light straight to the suprachiasmatic nucleus.

So whether light can set the body clock has no direct connection with whether the eyes can see anything. In some people who are completely blind, the body clock can still be set by light.

This is also why item 2 in section 3 (fix your wake-up time, at weekends as well) has to be paired with getting light in the morning.

## 3. The key point is that there is more than one clock

The liver, the gut, the heart and the fatty tissue each have their own set of peripheral clocks.

The master clock sets itself by light. **The peripheral clocks set themselves largely by when you eat.**

Under a normal routine, the two are in step. You see light in the day and you eat in the day, so the master clock and the peripheral clocks point at the same time. Which is why in ordinary life you never notice that they are two separate systems.

## 4. What a night shift messes up is not the lack of sleep, it is the two clocks coming apart

Someone on night shifts still sees light in the day, and still lives the social life of the daytime. So the master clock stays locked to the day-and-night cycle outside.

But you eat, work and turn your metabolism over in the small hours of the night in biological terms. The peripheral clocks get pulled the other way.

That state is called circadian misalignment. The problem is not how many fewer hours you slept. It is that the clocks across your body no longer agree with each other.

## 5. And you do not adapt to it

Some people think that in the long run a fixed run of night shifts has to be adaptable.

One review pooled six studies and used the rhythm of melatonin secretion to test people on long-term fixed night shifts. The melatonin rhythm is the most reliable measure of the state of the internal body clock.

The result is that fewer than 3% of people achieved "complete" adaptation. Fewer than a quarter reached "considerable" adaptation.

It makes no difference how many years you have done it. The reason is in the paragraph above. In the day you still see light, and you still live the social life of the daytime, and those cues go on pulling the master clock back.

Sources: Folkard S (2008). Do permanent night workers show circadian adjustment? A review based on the endogenous melatonin rhythm. Chronobiology International. <https://doi.org/10.1080/07420520802106835>

## 6. How to tell that the misalignment itself does the harm, rather than the lost sleep alone

This is the most worth-asking layer of the whole question. People on night shifts sleep less, eat badly, carry more stress and smoke more, and any of these could be the real cause.

The good thing is that this can be produced on its own in a laboratory.

The study by Scheer et al in 2009: ten adults lived in a laboratory for 10 days. The researchers set "one day" artificially at 28 hours, so that their eating and sleeping had to run through every hour of the body clock. **Four meals a day, with the calorie intake fixed.**

When behaviour was about 12 hours out of step with the body clock, the results were:

- Leptin fell 17% (leptin is the hormone that makes you feel full)
- Blood glucose rose 6%, and that was with insulin already up 22%
- The daily rhythm of cortisol turned right round
- Mean arterial pressure rose 3%
- Sleep efficiency fell 20%

The most important one of these: of the 8 people with enough data, 3 of them had post-meal blood glucose move into the prediabetes range, and it took 10 days in all.

Because the calorie intake was fixed and the experiment was controlled in a laboratory, these changes cannot be put down to "sleeping less" or to "eating more". **Misalignment on its own is enough to bring about these changes.**

Sources: Scheer FA, Hilton MF, Mantzoros CS, Shea SA (2009). Adverse metabolic and cardiovascular consequences of circadian misalignment. PNAS, 106(11), 4453-4458. <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0808180106>

## 7. The first thing you can change: move your eating into the daytime

Since the peripheral clocks set themselves by when you eat, there is one direction to try. Keep the night shift, but do not eat in the night.

One randomised controlled trial did this (registration number NCT02291952). Twenty healthy people simulated night shifts and were randomly put into two groups. One group followed the common habit of night-shift workers and ate both at night and in the day. The other group ate only in the day.

The report on the primary outcome says that in the group that ate at night, the central rhythm and the peripheral glucose rhythm were out of step, and glucose tolerance got worse. Neither of those two appeared in the group that ate only in the day.

The cardiovascular report (a secondary analysis of the same trial) says that in the group that ate at night, pNN50, a measure of cardiac vagal modulation, fell 25.7%, RMSSD fell 14.3%, LF/HF, which reflects the balance between the sympathetic and the vagus, rose 5.5%, and PAI-1, a clotting-promoting factor, rose 23.9%. None of these changes appeared in the group that ate only in the day.

**But this is 20 people, and it is a simulated night shift, not real night-shift workers.**

A systematic review that looked specifically for randomised trials of this kind searched six databases and found only 4 trials. The results are not tidy either. Some trials saw fasting insulin and insulin resistance improve, and some did not. For high-density lipoprotein, none of the four trials saw a change. For post-meal blood glucose, the version that fasted for 10.75 hours overnight did come out better than the version that ate during the night and then had a midnight snack. The authors of the review say themselves that there are too few trials to apply the result widely.

So that is all that can be said at present. **Concentrating your eating in the daytime as far as possible during night shifts is supported by a randomised trial, but the evidence is still thin.** It costs nothing and it is not hard to do, so if you want to try it, try it. There is a second thing to try, in the next section. Use light to move the whole body clock across.

Sources: Chellappa SL et al. (2021). Daytime eating prevents internal circadian misalignment and glucose intolerance in night work. Science Advances. <https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abg9910>；Chellappa SL et al. (2025). Daytime eating during simulated night work mitigates changes in cardiovascular risk factors: secondary analyses of a randomized controlled trial. Nature Communications. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-57846-y>；Wulandari F et al. (2026). Cardiometabolic status among shift workers under meal time regulation: A systematic review of randomized controlled trials. Nutrition and Health. <https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261464874>

## 8. Whether the clock can be moved across

All of that so far is "misalignment does harm". Going the other way, does it work to move the whole clock across to the night shift side?

**In theory it works, and it has been done. But the demand on light is one order of magnitude higher than most people think.**

The study by Czeisler et al in 1990: eight young men did a week of night shifts, and two conditions were compared.

Under the control condition, the lighting at night was ordinary indoor light, about 150 lux. The lux is the unit of illuminance, and an ordinary fluorescent-lit room is somewhere in the low hundreds. The result was that after six nights in a row, the lowest point of body temperature still fell at about half past three in the morning. The lowest point of body temperature is the standard measure of where the body clock is pointing. This says the body clock had not moved at all.

Under the treatment condition, the night got strong light of 7000 to 12000 lux, and the day was spent asleep in a setting close to pitch dark. After four days, the lowest point of body temperature had moved to close to three in the afternoon. That is the whole thing turned round.

Sources: Czeisler CA, Johnson MP, Duffy JF, Brown EN, Ronda JM, Kronauer RE (1990). Exposure to bright light and darkness to treat physiologic maladaptation to night work. New England Journal of Medicine, 322(18), 1253-1259. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199005033221801>

### All three are needed and none can be dropped

**First, the light at night has to be bright enough.** Working under ordinary fluorescent lights is the same as being in the "nothing happened" control group above. To have any effect you need a dedicated light therapy box.

**Second, sleeping in the day has to be strictly darkened.** Blackout curtains plus an eye mask. The experiment above did "strong light at night" and "close to pitch dark in the day" together, not one or the other.

**Third, wear sunglasses on the way home from work.** This is the one most easily left out, and leaving it out means most of the work is wasted.

Why the third one matters so much comes down to how light works. The same light, arriving at different times, pushes the body clock in opposite directions. Light **before** the lowest point of body temperature pushes the clock later. Light **after** it pulls the clock earlier.

Walking home at the end of a night shift puts you just after the lowest point of body temperature. **This morning light does the opposite of what a whole night in front of a light box does.**

### The laboratory manages it, ordinary life does not

The people in the laboratory did not leave the house for a week. In real life your days off have to be lived as daytime lives, collecting children, being with family, running errands. Go through it once and the clock gets pulled back. That is where the figure in section 5 comes from: fewer than 3% of people on long-term fixed night shifts adapt completely.

So in real life the aim is not usually to turn the clock all the way round. It is to move part of it and leave the clock sitting in the middle, awake enough at work and not completely wrecked on your days off.

### How much you can actually get out of it

A meta-analysis pooling 11 studies: light therapy gave shift workers about 32.5 minutes more total sleep time, and a sleep efficiency improvement of about 2.91. The effective dose was 900 to 6000 lux for at least 1 hour a night.

Another laboratory study used intermittent bright light. In the treatment group, melatonin secretion overlapped the scheduled sleep period by 4.90 hours, against only 2.62 hours in the control group (P=0.002). The drop in cognitive test scores that showed up in the control group on the last night shift was held down in the treatment group. The authors say outright in their conclusion that this approach still has to be tested on real night-shift workers.

Sources: Zhao C, Li N, Miao W, He Y, Lin Y (2025). A systematic review and meta-analysis on light therapy for sleep disorders in shift workers. Scientific Reports. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-83789-3>；Lammers-van der Holst HM et al. (2021). Efficacy of intermittent exposure to bright light for treating maladaptation to night work on a counterclockwise shift work rotation. Scandinavian Journal of Work, Environment & Health. <https://doi.org/10.5271/sjweh.3953>

### One thing nobody has tested

Whether getting the clock right brings cardiovascular risk down with it. **No trial has tested it.**

What is known is that misalignment does harm, and that came out of the experiment in section 6. That does not add up to "get it right and you are safe". And the days off pull the clock back again, and nobody has tested whether that going back and forth is good or bad either.

The two measures above end at metabolism and cardiovascular measures on one side and at sleep on the other. Neither reaches illness or death.
